## What is Lacuna?
Most of ancient literature has been lost to history. For example, we know Aristotle wrote an estimated 200 works, but we only have access to 31. The existence of these lost works is known to us through fragments, quotations, references, et cetera. 

Specialists devote their careers to collecting and annotating this evidence, but what they provide is often dense, paywalled, and generally inaccessible to nonacademics (for example: Brill's New Jacoby). A useful analogy is the difference between a book on the industrial revolution versus a Wikipedia article on it -- both serve useful functions, and are in fact complementary. Lacuna aims to serve the latter functionality, harnessing modern software and AI-assisted workflows to transform fragments and scattered references of lost works into living profiles, all displayed alongside known works on an interactive node graph.

## Interactive Node Graph (ING)
- ING refers to lost works as ghost works, represented in a pale blue color with dashed border.
- ING refers to known works as known works, represented in an olive green color with solid border. 
- ING displays ghost works and known works together, enabling spatial navigation. Click to enter.
- ING visually displays edges to represent relation (direct quote, commentary, et cetera).
- ING creates a visceral display of how much of a corpus has been lost, making the absences the focus.

## Lost Work Profile Synthesis
- Profiles are written to be deliberately dry and uninsightful (like Wikipedia).
- Profiles are written to be holistic and nuanced, containing an uncertainty system.
- Profiles include introductions, concepts, chapters, et cetera.
- Profile generation includes forced citations and verification loop.
- Profiles include transparent decision log, used both by human and AI.  

## Philosophy
- Commitment to factuality.
- Simplicity
- Transparency.
- Accessibility. 
- Delightful and intuitive user experience.
- Provide real intellectual value.

## Benefits
- Increased accessibility of domain to students, autodidacts, and curious peoples.
- Valuable open data layer (backbone of the project). Supports independent research, future digital humanities projects. 
- Less laborious and time consuming to deploy.
- Greater surface area of knowledge.
- Modern and intuitive user experience.
- Fully free and open source. 

## Risks
- Fragment epistemology (scoring evidence against criteria, leveraging AI’s nondeterministic logic).
- Drift and error (verification loop, decision log)
- Scholarly trust (dispositionally conservative attitude, transparency).
- Navigation at scale (filters, search, clustering).

## Roadmap 
- Milestone one (estimated three months):
  - Evidence ingestion, and deduplication workflow.

  - Structured data set of known and lost works.
  - Interactive node graph, including edge types.
  - Profile synthesis and decision log workflow.
  - Search, navigation, filters.
  - Begin with tragedians.
  - Evaluations. 
  - Public documentation.
  - Reach out to scholars, professors, or other relevant individuals. 
- Assess, and proceed to less trodden ground (Sanskrit, early chinese, more).

## About

I came up with the concept for Lacuna about six months ago. I was listening to some podcast, and offhand, the host or guest mentioned how the majority of ancient literature has been lost to history. This induced a deep level of sadness, and, as I continued to dwell on that feeling, it bloomed into the concept for Lacuna. You can still view the old repo and prototype, but consider it a stale artifact: https://github.com/akhilm06/Lacuna-OLD.

I just can't seem to get Lacuna out of my head, and because I'd like to dedicate a portion of my youth working on a project that contributes intellectually, I intend to work on Lacuna full time, beginning with foundational research, methodology, and technical structure to establish a high-value open source project.
